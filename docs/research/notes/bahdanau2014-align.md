# Setting

Problem class: sequence-to-sequence neural machine translation (English→French,
WMT'14). Input: a variable-length source sentence $x = (x_1, \dots, x_{T_x})$,
tokenized, 30k-word shortlist vocabulary, out-of-shortlist words mapped to
`[UNK]` (§4.1). Output: a variable-length target sentence
$y = (y_1, \dots, y_{T_y})$ generated by an RNN decoder via beam search (§4.2).
No image/calibration preconditions — this is the origin paper for the general
soft-attention mechanism, cited here as the historical root of
content-based attention later generalized to vision and to the
Transformer's dot-product attention (§3, Appendix A).

# Core idea

The paper replaces the single fixed-length context vector of the
RNN Encoder–Decoder (§2.1, Eq. 1–3) with a *distinct context vector $c_i$ per
output step*, computed as a weighted sum over all encoder annotations rather
than a single terminal hidden state. A bidirectional RNN (BiRNN) encodes the
source into annotations $h_j = [\overrightarrow{h_j}^\top; \overleftarrow{h_j}^\top]^\top$
(Eq. 7, §3.2) that each summarize the whole sentence with a local focus around
position $j$. At each decoder step $i$, an *alignment model* — a small
feedforward network — scores how well decoder state $s_{i-1}$ matches each
annotation $h_j$, the scores are normalized by softmax into weights
$\alpha_{ij}$, and the context vector is their weighted sum
$c_i = \sum_j \alpha_{ij} h_j$ (Eq. 5–6, §3.1). This is described explicitly as
computing an *expected annotation* under a soft-alignment distribution
(§3.1, "We can understand the approach... as computing an expected
annotation, where the expectation is over possible alignments"), and as
implementing "a mechanism of attention in the decoder" (§3.1) — one of the
earliest uses of the word "attention" in this technical sense.

# Assumptions

1. The alignment model is a single-layer MLP (not a bilinear/dot-product
   form) — a specific parametric choice (Appendix A.1.2), not implied by the
   general framework of §3, which is stated to work with any differentiable
   `a` (Appendix A.1: "a general framework where one can freely define...the
   alignment model $a$").
2. The annotation weights $\alpha_{ij}$ are treated as a *soft* (fully dense,
   differentiable) distribution over source positions rather than a discrete
   latent variable, specifically so that gradients backpropagate through the
   alignment computation jointly with the rest of the model (§3.1: "unlike in
   traditional machine translation, the alignment is not considered to be a
   latent variable").
3. Cost of the alignment model scales as $O(T_x \times T_y)$ per sentence
   pair — the authors note this explicitly as a design constraint motivating
   the cheap single-layer MLP form, and as a scalability caution beyond
   translation-length sequences ("this may limit the applicability of the
   proposed scheme to other tasks", §6.1).
4. Bidirectionality of the encoder is a soft requirement for good annotation
   quality: a unidirectional RNN tends to bias annotations toward recent
   (upstream) context only (§3.2), so a BiRNN is used to balance forward and
   backward context per position — not a strict requirement of the alignment
   mechanism itself, which is agnostic to how annotations are produced.

# Failure regime

- The baseline this paper targets (RNNencdec, single fixed-length context
  vector) degrades sharply as sentence length grows past training-set typical
  lengths (§1, citing Cho et al. 2014b) — Fig. 2 shows RNNencdec-30/50 BLEU
  dropping steadily past ~20-word sentences, while RNNsearch-50 shows "no
  performance deterioration even with sentences of length 50 or more" (§5.1).
- The proposed alignment mechanism itself is reported to have no
  length-degradation failure mode within the tested range (sentences up to
  ~50+ words, News-test-2014, 3003 sentences, §4.1, §5.1) — this is an
  empirical, not theoretical, guarantee.
- Rare/unknown words remain unresolved: the paper explicitly flags "one of
  challenges left for the future is to better handle unknown, or rare words"
  (§7) — attention does not fix out-of-vocabulary handling.
- Graves (2013)'s earlier alignment mechanism for handwriting synthesis
  constrained alignment position to move monotonically; the authors note this
  "is a severe limitation" for machine translation where "(long-distance)
  reordering is often needed" (§6.1) — i.e. any alignment model restricted to
  monotonic advance fails on reordering-heavy translation pairs (e.g.
  English↔German cited as an example).

# Numerical sensitivity

- Parameter initialization matters for the alignment model specifically:
  $W_a$ and $U_a$ are initialized from $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.001^2)$ (Appendix
  B.1), a notably smaller variance than the general weight-matrix
  initialization $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.01^2)$ used elsewhere — recurrent weight
  matrices are instead initialized as random orthogonal matrices. $v_a$ and
  all bias vectors are initialized to zero (Appendix B.1).
- $U_a h_j$ does not depend on the decoder step $i$, so it is precomputed
  once per source sentence and reused across all $T_y$ decoder steps — stated
  explicitly as a computational-cost mitigation (Appendix A.1.2), since the
  alignment model is evaluated $T_x \times T_y$ times per sentence pair.
- Model sizes used in the reported experiments (Appendix A.2.3): hidden layer
  size $n=1000$, word-embedding dimensionality $m=620$, maxout deep-output
  layer size $l=500$, alignment-model hidden units $n'=1000$.

# Applicability

- Use when: the source sequence is long relative to what a fixed-size vector
  can represent losslessly, and per-output-step relevance varies across
  source positions (translation, and by extension any seq2seq task with
  positional correspondence between input and output).
- Don't use when: sequence lengths make the $O(T_x T_y)$ alignment cost
  prohibitive without further approximation (flagged as an open scalability
  concern in §6.1) — a caveat the paper raises about applicability beyond
  translation-length sequences, later addressed differently by fixed-window
  local attention and by self-attention architectures (out of scope for this
  paper).
- Compared against: RNN Encoder–Decoder (Cho et al. 2014a / Sutskever et al.
  2014, single fixed-length context vector, §2.1) as the direct ablation
  baseline (RNNencdec vs RNNsearch, Table 1, Fig. 2); Graves (2013)'s
  monotonic Gaussian-mixture alignment mechanism for handwriting synthesis
  (§6.1) as a related but more restricted alignment mechanism; the
  phrase-based statistical MT system Moses as an external non-neural
  baseline (Table 1).

# Connections

- Builds on: RNN Encoder–Decoder architecture (Cho et al., 2014a; Sutskever
  et al., 2014, §2.1) — the alignment mechanism is proposed as an extension
  layered on top of this framework, not a replacement of it.
- Related but distinct: Graves (2013) handwriting-synthesis alignment
  (Gaussian-mixture, monotonic) — earlier use of a learned alignment weighting
  mechanism in a different domain, discussed and contrasted in §6.1.
- Enables (beyond this paper's scope, noted for concept-page framing): this
  is the paper that establishes learned, differentiable, soft content-based
  alignment/attention as a mechanism — the historical root that the
  attention-mechanism concept page traces forward to dot-product/multi-head
  self-attention (Vaswani et al. 2017) and to attention mechanisms in vision.
