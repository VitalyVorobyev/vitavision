# Setting

Joint pre-training of an image encoder and a text encoder from (image, text) pairs scraped from the public internet, with no per-image class labels at any point. Input: a dataset of 400 million (image, text) pairs constructed by the authors (called WIT, "WebImageText"), collected by querying for text co-occurring with images against a list of 500,000 search terms (base list = all words occurring ≥100 times in English Wikipedia, augmented with high-PMI bigrams, popular Wikipedia article titles, and WordNet synsets not already present), with results approximately class-balanced by capping at 20,000 pairs per query (§2.2). Output: two encoders — an image encoder $f_I$ and a text encoder $f_T$ — mapping into a shared embedding space via cosine similarity; at inference the text encoder is repurposed as a "hypernetwork" (§3.1.2, citing Ha et al. 2016) that synthesizes the weights of a zero-shot linear classifier from a list of class-name strings, with no gradient updates and no task-specific training data required.

# Core idea

CLIP (Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training) replaces per-image class prediction with a **contrastive matching objective over (image, text) pairs**, trained purely from noisy web text rather than crowd-labeled category IDs (§1, Fig. 1).

Given a minibatch of $N$ (image, text) pairs, the image encoder produces features $I_f = \text{image\_encoder}(I)$ and the text encoder produces $T_f = \text{text\_encoder}(T)$; each is linearly projected into a shared $d_e$-dimensional embedding space and $\ell_2$-normalized: $I_e = \ell_2(\,I_f W_i\,)$, $T_e = \ell_2(\,T_f W_t\,)$ (§2.3, Fig. 3 pseudocode). CLIP is trained to predict, out of the $N \times N$ possible pairings in the batch, which $N$ actually co-occurred, by maximizing the cosine similarity of the $N$ true (image, text) pairs while minimizing it for the $N^2-N$ incorrect pairings — a **symmetric cross-entropy loss** over the batch's scaled similarity matrix (§2.3):

```
logits = I_e @ T_e.T * exp(t)          # [N, N] scaled cosine similarities
labels = arange(N)
loss_i = cross_entropy(logits, labels, axis=0)   # image→text direction
loss_t = cross_entropy(logits, labels, axis=1)   # text→image direction
loss   = (loss_i + loss_t) / 2
```
(Figure 3, full pseudocode, §2.3.) The temperature $t$ (paper's notation; $\tau = \exp(-t)$ in the similarity scaling) is a **learned, log-parameterized scalar** initialized to the equivalent of 0.07 (following Wu et al. 2018) and clipped so the logits are never scaled by more than 100 — added "to prevent training instability" (§2.5). This batch-construction technique and objective is explicitly attributed to prior work, not claimed as novel: "multi-class N-pair loss" (Sohn 2016), popularized as InfoNCE (Oord et al. 2018), and adapted to (text, image) contrastive learning for medical imaging by Zhang et al. 2020 [ConVIRT] (§2.3).

**Why contrastive, not predictive captioning (Fig. 2).** The paper's first approach, similar to VirTex, jointly trained an image CNN and text transformer *from scratch to predict the exact caption* of an image (a generative/predictive objective). This scaled poorly: a 63M-parameter transformer language model — already using 2x the compute of its ResNet-50 image encoder — learned to recognize ImageNet classes **3x slower** than a much simpler baseline that instead predicts a bag-of-words (BoW) encoding of the same text (§2.3, Fig. 2). Swapping the BoW baseline's predictive objective for CLIP's contrastive objective gave **a further 4x efficiency improvement** in the rate of zero-shot ImageNet transfer (§2.3, Fig. 2 caption) — i.e., roughly 12x more compute-efficient than the original transformer-captioning baseline, though the paper states only the two individual 3x and 4x factors and does not itself multiply them into a headline "12x" figure (`?`, inferred by composing the two stated ratios, not asserted verbatim). The stated rationale: predicting the *exact* words of a caption is unnecessarily hard given the wide variety of co-occurring text, and contrastive objectives are reported elsewhere to learn better representations than equivalent predictive objectives at lower compute (§2.3, citing Tian et al. 2019; Chen et al. 2020a).

**No non-linear projection head.** Unlike Chen et al. 2020b [SimCLR], which popularized a non-linear MLP projection head between the representation and the contrastive embedding space, CLIP uses **only a linear projection** ($W_i$, $W_t$ above) from each encoder's representation to the shared embedding space; the paper reports no efficiency difference between linear and non-linear projections in its own ablation and speculates non-linear projections "may be co-adapted with details of current image-only self-supervised representation learning methods" (§2.3). CLIP also omits ConVIRT's random single-sentence sampling transform $t_u$ (most WIT captions are already single sentences) and simplifies the image augmentation $t_v$ to a single random square crop from a resized image — no other augmentation (§2.3).

**Dual encoders — two families each (§2.4):**
- *Image encoder, ResNet family.* A modified ResNet-50 (He et al. 2016a) base: ResNet-D stem/downsampling improvements (He et al. 2019) plus antialiased rect-2 blur pooling (Zhang 2019), with global average pooling replaced by an **attention-pooling** mechanism — a single layer of transformer-style multi-head QKV attention whose query is conditioned on the global-average-pooled image representation (§2.4). Scaled versions RN50x4, RN50x16, RN50x64 use ~4x/16x/64x the compute of RN50, following EfficientNet-style joint scaling of width, depth, *and* resolution together rather than any single dimension in isolation (§2.4, citing Tan & Le 2019) — "we use a simple baseline of allocating additional compute equally to increasing the width, depth, and resolution of the model" (§2.4).
- *Image encoder, ViT family.* The Vision Transformer (Dosovitskiy et al. 2020), followed closely, with one architectural addition — an extra layer normalization applied to the combined patch + position embeddings before the transformer — and a different initialization scheme (§2.4). Variants trained: ViT-B/32, ViT-B/16, ViT-L/14, plus ViT-L/14@336px (the ViT-L/14 model additionally fine-tuned for one epoch at 336px input resolution, following FixRes; Touvron et al. 2019). "Unless otherwise specified, all results reported in this paper as 'CLIP' use this [ViT-L/14@336px] model which we found to perform best" (§2.5).
- *Text encoder.* A Transformer (Vaswani et al. 2017) with the GPT-2-style architecture modifications of Radford et al. 2019, base size **63M parameters, 12 layers, 512-wide, 8 attention heads** — verified verbatim in §2.4. Operates on lower-cased byte-pair-encoded (BPE) text, 49,152-token vocabulary (Sennrich et al. 2015), max sequence length capped at 76 for compute efficiency, sequence bracketed with `[SOS]`/`[EOS]` tokens; the highest-layer activation at the `[EOS]` position is taken as the text feature, layer-normalized, then linearly projected into the shared embedding space. Masked self-attention is used (not bidirectional) specifically to preserve the option of initializing from, or adding, a language-modeling objective later — left as future work (§2.4). Only the *width* of the text encoder is scaled with model size (proportional to the ResNet's width increase); depth is not scaled, because CLIP's performance was found to be less sensitive to text-encoder capacity (§2.4).

**Zero-shot classification mechanics (§3.1.2).** To classify an image against a set of candidate class names, both the image and every candidate name (embedded via a template, see below) are encoded; cosine similarities are scaled by $\tau$ and passed through a softmax. "Note that this prediction layer is a multinomial logistic regression classifier with L2-normalized inputs, L2-normalized weights, no bias, and temperature scaling" (§3.1.2). The text encoder is reinterpreted as a hypernetwork that generates the weights of this linear classifier from natural-language class descriptions; the classifier is cached once per dataset and reused across predictions to amortize its cost (§3.1.2).

**Prompt engineering and ensembling (§3.1.4, Fig. 4).** Using the template "`A photo of a {label}.`" instead of a bare class-name string improves ImageNet accuracy by 1.3% — bridging the distribution gap between single-word labels and the mostly full-sentence captions in WIT, and helping resolve polysemy (e.g. "boxer" as dog breed vs. athlete; "crane" as bird vs. construction equipment) (§3.1.4). Task-specific prompt customization helps further (e.g. "`{label}, a type of pet`" for Oxford-IIIT Pets, "`a type of food`" for Food101, "`a satellite photo of a {label}`" for satellite datasets, quoting text/numbers for OCR tasks). Ensembling multiple prompt-generated zero-shot classifiers (e.g. varying "big"/"small" context) in **embedding space** (not probability space, so the amortized cost stays that of a single classifier) further improves performance; on ImageNet, ensembling **80 different context prompts** improves accuracy by an additional 3.5% over the single default prompt. "When considered together, prompt engineering and ensembling improve ImageNet accuracy by almost 5%" (§3.1.4) — comparable to a 4x compute increase under the baseline zero-shot method, but "free" once amortized (Fig. 4 caption).

# Claimed contributions

- C1: A new 400M-pair (image, text) dataset (WIT) collected from the public internet, motivated because existing datasets (MS-COCO, Visual Genome, filtered YFCC100M) are too small (~100K–15M images) to test the natural-language-supervision hypothesis at scale (§1, §2.2: "we constructed a new dataset of 400 million (image, text) pairs collected [from] a variety of publicly available sources on the Internet").
- C2: "the simple pre-training task of predicting which caption goes with which image is an efficient and scalable way to learn SOTA image representations from scratch" on this dataset (Abstract).
- C3: Zero-shot transfer of the resulting model to new tasks/datasets via natural-language class descriptions, with no dataset-specific training: "we match the accuracy of the original ResNet-50 on ImageNet zero-shot without needing to use any of the 1.28 million training examples it was trained on" (Abstract); benchmarked "on over 30 different existing computer vision datasets, spanning tasks such as OCR, action recognition in videos, geo-localization, and many types of fine-grained object classification" (Abstract).
- C4: A demonstration that transfer performance is "a smoothly predictable function of compute" across a series of 8 models spanning almost 2 orders of magnitude of compute (§1, confirmed quantitatively in §3.1.5/Fig. 9 as a log-log linear trend across a 44x compute range).
- C5: A finding that "zero-shot CLIP models are much more robust than equivalent accuracy supervised ImageNet models," proposed as evidence that zero-shot evaluation of task-agnostic models better represents true model capability than in-distribution accuracy (§1, elaborated in §3.3).
- C6: Release of code and pre-trained weights at github.com/OpenAI/CLIP (Abstract).

# Assumptions

1. (Hard) The pre-training signal is *pairing structure*, not per-image semantic labels — the method needs (image, text) pairs at scale, not curated class taxonomies; over-fitting is treated as a non-issue purely because of dataset size ("Due to the large size of our pre-training dataset, over-fitting is not a major concern," §2.3), which is itself an assumption that has not been separately ablated in the note's read of the paper.
2. (Hard) Zero-shot classification requires that target classes be namable/describable in natural language and that the dataset expose those names — the paper notes several datasets (Flowers102, GTSRB in their released form) omit a class-name mapping entirely, "preventing zero-shot transfer entirely" (§3.1.4).
3. (Soft) The learned temperature must be clipped (logits scaled by ≤100) to avoid training instability; this is stated as an empirically necessary constraint, not derived (§2.5).
4. (Soft) Batch size is large by construction (32,768) — the $N \times N$ contrastive matching objective's effective negative-set size is tied directly to batch size, the same batch-size dependency documented for image-only contrastive SSL methods (§2.5; cf. chen2020-simclr's identical in-batch-negative dependency).
5. (Soft) Zero-shot performance is implicitly assumed representative of "true" zero-shot capability, but the paper itself flags that "we repeatedly queried performance on full validation sets to guide the development of CLIP," which is not a realistic zero-shot deployment scenario (§6, Limitations).

# Failure regime

- **Fine-grained, abstract, and counting tasks.** "CLIP also struggles with more abstract and systematic tasks such as counting the number of objects in an image" (§6); zero-shot CLIP underperforms a ResNet-50 linear-probe baseline by over 10% on Flowers102 and FGVCAircraft (§3.1.5), and is weak on differentiating models of cars, species of flowers, and variants of aircraft relative to task-specific models (§6).
- **Truly out-of-distribution data.** Zero-shot CLIP achieves only 88% accuracy on MNIST handwritten digits — "an embarrassingly simple baseline of logistic regression on raw pixels outperforms zero-shot CLIP" — despite strong performance on digitally-rendered text (Rendered SST2), because near-duplicate and semantic nearest-neighbor retrieval find "almost no images that resemble MNIST digits" in WIT (§6). The paper concludes this shows CLIP "does little to address the underlying problem of brittle generalization," instead hoping large/varied pre-training data makes most evaluation data effectively in-distribution — "a naive assumption that, as MNIST demonstrates, is easy to violate" (§6).
- **Specialized/complex/abstract tasks generally.** Zero-shot CLIP is "quite weak" on satellite image classification (EuroSAT, RESISC45), lymph-node tumor detection (PatchCamelyon), synthetic-scene object counting (CLEVRCounts), German traffic-sign recognition (GTSRB), and distance-to-nearest-car estimation (KITTI Distance) — tasks non-expert humans can perform reasonably well on several of (counting, satellite imagery, traffic signs), "suggesting significant room for improvement" (§3.1.5).
- **Zero-shot ceiling relative to fully supervised.** For most datasets, zero-shot performance underperforms a fully-supervised linear classifier on the same feature space "by 10% to 25%" (§3.1.5, Fig. 8) — only 5 of 27 datasets (STL10, CIFAR10, Food101, OxfordPets, Caltech101) come within ≤3 points of their fully-supervised linear-probe counterpart, all of them cases where both zero-shot and supervised accuracy already exceed 90%.
- **Supervised adaptation trades away robustness.** Fitting an L2-regularized logistic-regression classifier on CLIP features on the ImageNet training set raises ImageNet accuracy by 9.2% (to 85.4%, tying the 2018 Mahajan et al. SOTA) but *decreases* average accuracy under natural distribution shift: -4.7% on ImageNet-R, -3.8% on ObjectNet, -2.8% on ImageNet-Sketch, -1.9% on ImageNet-A, with the only clear gain concentrated on ImageNetV2 (which shares ImageNet's creation process) (§3.3, Fig. 14).
- **Zero-shot-to-few-shot dip.** Adding a small number of labeled examples and fitting a linear classifier on CLIP features initially *underperforms* the zero-shot classifier before eventually surpassing it around 4-shot on average — "a counter-intuitive drop in performance when transitioning from a zero-shot to a few-shot setting," attributed to CLIP not directly optimizing for few-shot performance, unlike human learners who show a large zero-to-one-shot jump (§6, §3.1.5 Fig. 6, §4).

# Numerical sensitivity

**Dataset construction (§2.2):** WIT = 400M (image, text) pairs; base query list = words occurring ≥100 times in English Wikipedia, augmented with high-PMI bigrams, popular article titles, WordNet synsets; up to 20,000 pairs kept per query for approximate class balance; total word count comparable to GPT-2's WebText training set. Contrast: MS-COCO/Visual Genome ≈100K photos each; filtered YFCC100M ≈15M photos (shrunk 6x from the full 100M by keeping only English natural-language titles/descriptions).

**Loss/temperature (§2.3, §2.5):** symmetric cross-entropy over an $N \times N$ scaled-cosine-similarity matrix; scaling factor $\exp(t)$ with $t$ a learned log-parameterized scalar, initialized to the equivalent of $\tau_0 = 0.07$ (Wu et al. 2018 convention), and clipped so the logits are never scaled by more than 100 — "which we found necessary to prevent training instability" (§2.5).

**Training configuration (§2.5):** 5 ResNets (RN50, RN101, RN50x4, RN50x16, RN50x64) + 3 ViTs (ViT-B/32, ViT-B/16, ViT-L/14) trained for 32 epochs each; Adam with decoupled weight decay (applied to all non-gain/bias weights) and a cosine learning-rate schedule; initial hyperparameters set via grid/random search + manual tuning on RN50 at 1 epoch, then heuristically adapted for larger models; minibatch size **32,768**; mixed precision, gradient checkpointing, half-precision Adam statistics, half-precision stochastically-rounded text-encoder weights, and sharded pairwise-similarity computation (each GPU computes only its local batch's subset) used to fit training in memory. Largest ResNet (RN50x64): 18 days on 592 V100 GPUs. Largest ViT (ViT-L/14): 12 days on 256 V100 GPUs; ViT-L/14@336px is that model fine-tuned 1 additional epoch at 336px resolution (FixRes-style, Touvron et al. 2019) and is the default "CLIP" model referenced elsewhere in the paper unless stated otherwise.

**Zero-shot proxy-task framing (§3.1.2):** "every step of CLIP pre-training can be viewed as optimizing the performance of a randomly created proxy to a computer vision dataset which contains 1 example per class and has 32,768 total classes" — i.e. the per-step effective "class count" equals the minibatch size.

**Efficiency ablation, Fig. 2 (§2.3):** transformer-LM captioning baseline (63M params, 2x the ResNet-50 encoder's compute) learns ImageNet recognition 3x *slower* than a BoW-prediction baseline; swapping BoW's predictive objective for CLIP's contrastive objective gives a further 4x efficiency improvement in zero-shot ImageNet transfer rate. (Composing the two ratios into ~12x vs. the original transformer-captioning baseline is this note's arithmetic, not a number the paper states directly — marked `?`.)

**Prompt engineering (§3.1.4, Fig. 4):** default template "`A photo of a {label}.`" — +1.3% ImageNet accuracy over bare class names; ensembling 80 context prompts on ImageNet — additional +3.5%; combined prompt engineering + ensembling — "almost 5%" ImageNet accuracy gain, described as comparable to a 4x compute increase under the non-engineered baseline.

**Headline zero-shot vs. prior art (Table 1, §3.1.3):** Visual N-Grams (Li et al. 2017) vs. CLIP: aYahoo 72.4 → **98.4**; ImageNet 11.5 → **76.2**; SUN 23.0 → **58.5**. ImageNet zero-shot top-5 accuracy ≈95%, matching Inception-V4. CLIP was trained on a dataset "10x larger," a vision model requiring "nearly 100x more compute per prediction," and "likely... over 1000x" more training compute than Visual N-Grams (§3.1.3) — these multipliers are the paper's own qualitative-comparison caveats, not controlled ablations.

**27-dataset zero-shot vs. linear-probe baseline (§3.1.5, Fig. 5):** zero-shot CLIP beats a fully-supervised logistic-regression classifier on canonical ResNet-50 features on 16/27 datasets; >20% margin in CLIP's favor on Stanford Cars and Food101; >10% margin against CLIP on Flowers102 and FGVCAircraft; STL10 reaches 99.3% ("a new state of the art despite not using any training examples"); Kinetics700 +14.5% and UCF101 +7.7% for CLIP over the ResNet-50 baseline (action recognition).

**Data efficiency (§3.1.5, Fig. 7):** effective zero-shot data efficiency (labeled examples/class a same-feature-space linear classifier needs to match zero-shot) ranges from <1 to 184 per class; half of datasets need <5 examples/class (median 5.4); mean 20.8 (skewed by the ~20% of datasets needing many examples); Flowers102 and EuroSAT underperform even a 1-shot classifier; on ImageNet, zero-shot CLIP matches a 16-shot linear classifier on the same features.

**Zero-shot ceiling (§3.1.5, Fig. 8):** correlation between zero-shot and fully-supervised linear-probe accuracy across datasets = **0.82** (p < $10^{-6}$); linear-regression slope estimating zero-shot-improvement-per-1%-supervised-improvement = **1.28** (95% CI [0.93, 1.79]); zero-shot underperforms supervised by 10–25% on most datasets, approaches within ≤3 points on only 5/27 (all >90% accuracy on both axes).

**Compute scaling (§3.1.5, Fig. 9):** average zero-shot error across 39 evals / 36 datasets follows a log-log linear trend across a **44x** range of compute spanning the 5 ResNet CLIP models.

**Linear-probe representation quality (§3.2, Figs. 10–11):** on Kornblith et al. 2019's 12-dataset suite, small CLIP models (RN50, RN101) underperform ImageNet-21K-trained BiT-M and EfficientNet-family models at similar compute, but the largest CLIP model (RN50x64) slightly outperforms the best existing model (Noisy Student EfficientNet-L2) on both overall score and compute efficiency; CLIP ViTs are ~3x more compute-efficient than CLIP ResNets; best overall model (ViT-L/14@336px) beats the best existing model by **+2.6%** average on the 12-dataset suite. On the broader 27-dataset suite, all CLIP models beat all evaluated systems in compute efficiency and the margin widens to **+5%** average; CLIP beats Noisy Student EfficientNet-L2 on **21/27** datasets, with the largest gains on OCR (SST2, HatefulMemes), geo-localization/scene recognition (Country211, SUN397), video action recognition (Kinetics700, UCF101), and fine-grained car/traffic-sign recognition — e.g. **+14.7%** on GTSRB. CLIP underperforms EfficientNet specifically on ImageNet itself, and slightly on CIFAR10/100 (attributed partly to CLIP's lack of scale-based data augmentation) and on PatchCamelyon/CLEVRCounts (where both approaches score low).

**Robustness to natural distribution shift (§3.3, Figs. 13–15):** 7 natural-shift datasets (ImageNetV2, ImageNet-Sketch, Youtube-BB, ImageNet-Vid, ObjectNet, ImageNet-A, ImageNet-R). A ResNet-101 makes 5x as many errors on these vs. the ImageNet validation set. Zero-shot CLIP shrinks the "robustness gap" (accuracy under shift vs. accuracy predicted from ImageNet accuracy) by **up to 75%**. Adapting CLIP to ImageNet via a linear probe raises ImageNet accuracy by **+9.2%** to **85.4%** (tying Mahajan et al. 2018's 2018 SOTA) but decreases shift accuracy on 4/7 datasets (-4.7% ImageNet-R, -3.8% ObjectNet, -2.8% ImageNet-Sketch, -1.9% ImageNet-A); using per-dataset custom zero-shot classifiers (vs. pooling ImageNet-class predictions) improves average effective robustness by an additional 5%, and ObjectNet accuracy specifically by +2.3%.

**Limitations, quantified (§6):** paper estimates "around a 1000x increase in compute is required for zero-shot CLIP to reach overall state-of-the-art performance" and calls this infeasible with current (2021) hardware; at one image/second, iterating through the 12.8 billion images seen over 32 training epochs (400M pairs × 32 epochs) would take **405 years** — used to illustrate CLIP's poor data efficiency, compensated for by scale of supervision rather than solved; zero-shot MNIST accuracy 88%, below a raw-pixel logistic-regression baseline (exact baseline number not given in the excerpted text, `?`).

# Applicability

- Use when: labeled data for the target task/domain is scarce or absent, but a natural-language description of the target classes exists — CLIP's classifier is synthesized from text, not fit from labeled examples.
- Use when: broad task-agnostic transfer to many unseen datasets/tasks is more valuable than best-possible performance on one fixed, well-labeled task.
- Use when: robustness to distribution shift matters more than squeezing out the last few points of in-distribution accuracy — zero-shot CLIP is reported far more robust to natural distribution shift than an ImageNet-adapted classifier at matched in-distribution accuracy.
- Don't use when: the task requires fine-grained discrimination among visually similar, textually under-specified classes (car models, aircraft variants, flower species) where CLIP's zero-shot performance can trail a simple supervised linear probe by >10%.
- Don't use when: the task is abstract/systematic (counting) or truly outside CLIP's pre-training distribution (e.g. handwritten-digit-style abstractions) — CLIP does not solve brittle generalization, it relies on its pre-training set being broad enough to make most evaluation data "in-distribution."
- Don't use when: open-ended generative output (not a fixed candidate-class list) is required — CLIP's zero-shot classifier can only choose among a supplied set of text candidates, unlike a captioning model (§6).
- Compared against (within paper): Visual N-Grams (Li et al. 2017) as the closest prior zero-shot-transfer baseline (§3.1.3); a supervised logistic-regression classifier on canonical ResNet-50 features, few-shot logistic regression on various public models' features (BiT-M, SimCLRv2, and others), and the best publicly available ImageNet models (EfficientNet family, BiT, ViT, MoCo, Instagram-pretrained ResNeXt, SimCLRv2, BYOL) for linear-probe representation quality (§3.1.5, §3.2, Fig. 10).

# Provenance

- Abstract: "the simple pre-training task of predicting which caption goes with which image is an efficient and scalable way to learn SOTA image representations from scratch on a dataset of 400 million (image, text) pairs"; "we match the accuracy of the original ResNet-50 on ImageNet zero-shot without needing to use any of the 1.28 million training examples."
- §1 (Introduction): "a simplified version of ConVIRT trained from scratch... which we call CLIP, for Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training"; "we study the scalability of CLIP by training a series of eight models spanning almost 2 orders of magnitude of compute"; Fig. 1 caption (approach summary diagram).
- §2.1 (Natural Language Supervision): motivation for learning from text vs. crowd-labeled data.
- §2.2 (Creating a Sufficiently Large Dataset): WIT construction — 500,000-query list, 20,000-pairs-per-query cap, comparison to MS-COCO/Visual Genome/YFCC100M scale; footnote 1 (query-list construction detail).
- §2.3 (Selecting an Efficient Pre-Training Method), Fig. 2, Fig. 3: predictive-captioning vs. BoW vs. contrastive efficiency ablation (3x, then a further 4x); $N \times N$ pairing objective; symmetric cross-entropy; pseudocode (image_encoder/text_encoder, $I_f$/$T_f$, $W_i$/$W_t$, $I_e$/$T_e$, `logits = I_e @ T_e.T * exp(t)`, `loss = (loss_i+loss_t)/2`); linear-only projection (vs. Bachman 2019/Chen 2020b non-linear projection); dropped ConVIRT $t_u$ transform; simplified $t_v$ (random square crop only).
- §2.4 (Choosing and Scaling a Model): ResNet-D + blur-pooling + attention-pooling modifications; ViT with added pre-transformer layernorm; text Transformer "63M-parameter 12-layer 512-wide model with 8 attention heads"; BPE 49,152 vocab; max sequence length 76; `[SOS]`/`[EOS]` tokens; width/depth/resolution joint scaling (citing Tan & Le 2019); text-encoder width-only scaling.
- §2.5 (Training): 5 ResNets + 3 ViTs, 32 epochs; Adam + decoupled weight decay + cosine schedule; temperature init 0.07 (Wu et al. 2018), clipped at 100x; batch size 32,768; mixed precision, gradient checkpointing, half-precision Adam stats, sharded similarity computation; RN50x64 training cost (18 days / 592 V100s), ViT-L/14 training cost (12 days / 256 V100s); ViT-L/14@336px definition and default-model statement.
- §3.1.1–3.1.2 (Zero-Shot Transfer, Motivation + Mechanics): zero-shot-as-task-generalization framing; hypernetwork interpretation of the text encoder (citing Ha et al. 2016; Lei Ba et al. 2015; Elhoseiny et al. 2013); "1 example per class and has 32,768 total classes" proxy-task framing; classifier caching.
